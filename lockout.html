The 2012 Lockout

Author

Andrew Lucas

The season that almost wasn’t.

113 days of labor war. 510 games erased. A 48-game sprint from January to April.

The 2012–13 NHL lockout, and what the shot data shows.

What Happened

At 11:59 p.m. on September 15, 2012, the NHL’s collective bargaining agreement expired — and the owners locked out the players. It was the third lockout under commissioner Gary Bettman, and the fight was over the same fault line as always: money. Players had been taking roughly 57% of hockey-related revenue — a pool worth about $3.3 billion a year — while owners pushed for a 50–50 divide and a tighter salary cap.

The season was supposed to open on October 11. Instead, the calendar melted away. Preseason games canceled first, then regular-season games in waves, then the Winter Classic — the league’s outdoor showcase — and the All-Star Weekend. In total, 510 regular-season games never happened: 41.5% of the schedule.

Talks sputtered, broke down over the winter holiday, and nearly ended in court when the union considered disclaiming interest. Then, after a 16-hour bargaining session brokered by federal mediator Scot Beckenbaugh, a tentative deal landed at 4:45 a.m. on January 6, 2013 — 113 days after the lockout began. The new CBA split revenue 50–50, limited contract lengths, and was ratified within the week.

The Sprint

The deal resurrected a 48-game season that ran from January 19 to April 28, 2013 — 99 days, roughly a game every other day. With no time to waste, the league scrapped all inter-conference games to cut travel and packed the schedule with back-to-backs after a one-week training camp.

The compressed chaos suited Chicago. The Blackhawks opened the season with 24 consecutive games without a regulation loss — an NHL record — and went on to win the Cup in June. The rest of the league simply tried to survive the treadmill.

By the Numbers

What did half a season do to the way the game was played? Almost nothing, at least per game. The shortened season produced 58.3 shots per game versus 59.5 the year before — a 2% dip — and scoring was essentially frozen at 5.30 goals per game in both seasons.

The lockout cut the quantity of hockey, not its character. Rate stats — the ones that describe how a game actually looks — shrugged off the disruption entirely.

The Takeaway

  1. The calendar shrank; the game didn’t. Shot and goal rates per game were nearly identical on either side of the lockout.
  2. Half the sample. Any comparison involving 2012-13 rests on 720 games instead of 1,230 — read raw totals carefully.
  3. Only a pandemic matched it. Across 19 seasons, only the COVID interruptions produced a comparable hole in the schedule.

The ice didn’t change when the lawyers left the room. Hockey, it turns out, is remarkably resistant to interruptions — as long as there are still games to play.
